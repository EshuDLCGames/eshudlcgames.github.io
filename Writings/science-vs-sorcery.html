Prologue: The Key of Light

Japan, 2016

Shadows fluttered like a flock of ravens under the moon.

Deep in a forest the maps had forgotten, an old temple sat among the cedars, its roof furred with moss, its lanterns long cold. Pilgrims had stopped coming a century ago. No one who wandered past would have guessed that behind the altar, under three tons of carved stone, an elevator dropped four hundred feet into the rock, to a facility wired with more security than most embassies.

It belonged to the Clerics.

For two thousand years the Clerics had hunted witches. Lately there had been few left to hunt. Ni-Ko's father had killed the last one anyone knew of, twenty-five years ago, before Dais was born. The order's work now was guarding things: relics, books, artifacts that could not be trusted to museums. The greatest of them sat in a vault at the bottom of the shaft. They called it the Key of Light. Nobody living knew what it opened. They only knew that their family had sworn, two millennia ago, that every last one of them would die before it left their keeping.

The oath belonged to the High Priest, and with the oath came the sword. The Shield of Light. Only the head of the clan could touch its handle and live. Tonight it hung at the hip of Ni-Ko Sojaki, twenty-nine years old and High Priestess for three of them, and eight warriors stood sworn to her. Her younger brother was one of them.

At 2:14 in the morning, the ground shook.

It wasn't an earthquake. Earthquakes didn't come in a rhythm. The alarm went up in the base, and a squad went down the corridor toward the elevator to see what was wrong.

Dais heard the shots over the comm. Then the screaming. Then a heavy, patient pounding, like a fist on a door. Then nothing at all.

He took two of Ni-Ko's guards and ran.

The elevator doors were turning to stone.

He stood there and watched it happen, because there was nothing else to do. Gray crept across the brushed steel like frost across a window, and then out across the wall around it, eating paint and conduit and the warning signs. Dais keyed his radio and called for every gun in the building. In under a minute the corridor was packed with troops in Kevlar, rifles and shotguns trained on a door that was no longer a door.

Dais drew his own guns: a matched pair of custom pistols, rapid-fire and heavier than they looked. Behind the stone, something was coming down the shaft. It sounded like boulders being dropped from a great height and smashing against the bottom. One. Another. Another. Then silence, and then the doors shuddered as something struck them from the inside. Once. Twice. On the third blow the wall gave way, and they poured out into the light. Eight of them, maybe nine. Figures of rough stone, broad as trucks, with no faces at all.

The corridor opened fire.

It sounded like the end of the world and did almost nothing. Rounds chipped at the creatures' hides like chisels on a cliff. Some bounced off and ricocheted back down the hall. The shotguns cracked off fist-sized chunks. The heavy rifles did a little better. None of it was enough. The stone things walked into the gunfire and through the men holding the guns, and they tore the soldiers apart like wet paper.

Dais got out because Kilik grabbed his collar and pulled.

They ran for the armory, the only place in the base with weapons heavier than what had just failed them. Dais slapped his palm on the scanner. It lit, considered him, and turned red. Unidentifiable reading. Please try again.

There was blood on the glass. He hadn't felt the cut. He wiped the panel and his hand on his trousers and tried again, and the light turned green, and the three-foot door rolled aside.

He turned to wave Kilik in and saw three dogs made of rock loping down the hallway behind him, gaining fast.

Dais raised his pistols and found Kilik's eyes. Kilik nodded once and threw himself sideways, rolling, coming up with the shotgun already leveled. They fired together, and the dogs came apart into gravel and dust that skidded to a stop at Kilik's boots. He got up, dusted off his knees, and walked in. The door sealed behind them.

They were pulling crates open when the first impact hit the door.

Three feet of steel bowed inward like the lid of a can. The second blow shook the whole base. Racks toppled, crates avalanched, and something heavy and square caught Dais across the side of the head.

The room went dark.

He woke to his sister's face above him, streaked with dust. The Shield of Light was in her left hand. She hauled him out of the wreckage, and although he felt like he'd been run over, nothing seemed to be broken.

"They took the Key," Ni-Ko said. "We're going after it."

Kilik lay against the far wall. He wasn't moving. He would never move again.

"How many are left?" Dais asked.

She looked away. "You and me. Jacob and Jared. They're waiting outside."

He made himself ask the question he didn't want answered. "Is it a witch?"

"I don't know." Her grip tightened on the sword. "If it is, she's stronger than anything in the stories. Come on."

He grabbed a box of experimental explosive rounds and a satchel of charges and ran after her. On the way out he saw another hand in the rubble, still and pale. He didn't stop to see whose it was.

One witch couldn't do all this, he thought, thumbing rounds into his magazines as he ran. Not even the old legends tell of anything like this. Whatever was waiting for them topside, it wasn't in any book the Clerics owned.

They fought their way up, and Ni-Ko went first.

Dais had trained with his sister his whole life and still wasn't ready for it. The Shield of Light moved in her hands like it had a will of its own, and anything it touched simply stopped. Stone soldiers froze mid-stride and collapsed into heaps of dead rock. Dais and the twins barely had to fire. Jared swept the halls behind her with his war-hammer Gatling gun. Jacob, the finest swordsman Dais had ever seen, finished whatever twitched with his laser-edged twin katanas. They left a trail of rubble from the vault to the stars.

A man was waiting for them in the temple courtyard.

He wore a long robe and a heavy hood, and he leaned on a tall white cane. A serpent of stone was coiled around its length, its fangs closed over a crystal at the top. As they watched, the serpent moved. Its coils tightened. Its head turned toward them. It swallowed the crystal whole, and it hissed.

The man walked forward. Under the hood there was only darkness. Ni-Ko held her ground with the sword raised, the blade burning white, and Dais could see her hands shaking.

"You know what I am," the hooded man said. "Don't you, child?"

Ni-Ko nodded.

"Good." Something in the dark of the hood seemed to smile. "I am Aleric, Grand Wizard of the Earth, Fifth Disciple of Ufur. I have come to take back what your family has so diligently kept for me."

Ni-Ko's voice came out steady. "If you are who you say you are, you know our contract says nothing about returning it. The Key was given to my family two thousand years ago. Every one of us swore to die before it left our care." She glanced back at her brother and the twins. "We intend to keep that oath."

"Child." His patience slipped. "Do you even know what it is? Do you know what you're about to die for?"

Then it was back, the smile. "Very well. Have it your way."

He raised the cane and drove it into the ground.

Stone spread from the point like ink in water. The grass around it grayed and stiffened, and then the stone grass came for Ni-Ko, blades of it launching from the ground like a hail of needles. She leaped clear and the storm turned to follow her. She cleared it again and came down bleeding.

Jared opened up with the Gatling gun. Before the first rounds reached the wizard, a pillar of rock thrust up out of the courtyard and took them. Jared circled, and the pillar circled with him.

Dais went the other way to flank, firing as he ran. A hand rose out of the stone to stop his bullets. It didn't burst out of the ground; it poured up out of it, as if the courtyard had turned to liquid and the hand were being sculpted from it. An arm followed, and a shoulder, and a head.

What stood up out of the earth was thirty feet tall and made of dark granite that glittered in the moonlight. Its eyes glowed like coals at the bottom of a furnace.

Dais put two shots into its face. It raised a fist the size of a car and took them like raindrops. Then it laced both hands together over its head. Dais emptied his explosive rounds into its legs. They didn't so much as scratch it. The fists came down. The ground jumped, and Dais left his feet, and before he could land, a hand swept him out of the air and hurled him into the temple wall.

One of his pistols skittered away. When his eyes cleared, the giant stood over him with its fist raised.

"OTHIUS, WAIT!"

The giant turned its head at the sound of its name. That was how Dais saw the sword coming. Ni-Ko's blade passed through the giant's wrist as if it were smoke. The fist fell, struck the ground, and crumbled to dust.

Othius staggered back. Ni-Ko was already at Dais's side, pulling him up, but the giant came on. Dais aimed at the ground at its feet, not at its body, and fired four times. The charges went off in a chain and ripped the courtyard open under Othius's heels. It went down hard, and they ran.

Across the courtyard, Jacob was fighting the wizard alone.

Jacob had never lost a fight that Dais had seen. He could not lay a blade on this man. Aleric turned every cut aside with the cane, unhurried, almost bored. Dais left his sister to finish the giant and went to help. He couldn't get a clean shot with the two of them so close, so he waded in. It made no difference. They came at the wizard from two sides, and he handled them both.

Then Jacob lunged for the throat. Aleric came apart into smoke and re-formed behind him. He took Jacob by the back of the neck, almost gently, and looked across at Dais. He smiled. He murmured something in a language Dais had never heard.

Jacob turned to stone where he stood.

Dais raised his gun to fire and felt its weight change. He looked down. The pistol in his hand was solid rock.

Ni-Ko had brought the giant down for good. She was running to him when an arrow took her in the right shoulder. She spun and knocked the next one out of the air an inch from her heart, then threw herself flat as three more hissed past. Where they struck the ground, thorned vines burst up and lunged at her. Holding her bleeding shoulder with one hand, she hacked through them with the other while Dais scrambled for his lost pistol.

When the last vine fell, Ni-Ko's eyes went to the shadows. Dais followed her gaze.

Something rose out of the dark on wings like a hummingbird's, glittering like stardust in the moonlight. It was a woman, and she was beautiful, and she was shooting at them from a bow strapped to her forearm.

Three arrows came down like lightning. Ni-Ko's blade turned all three. Then the archer drew a long, thin fencing sword and fell on her like a comet. Their blades locked. For a moment they were face to face: Ni-Ko dirty, bloody, teeth bared, and the winged woman smiling. Her skin had a faint glow, like a fairy's in an old painting. She wore skintight black leather, and her wings beat so fast they blurred.

"I am Silth," she said. She looked past Ni-Ko at Dais, and she winked.

Ni-Ko shoved her off balance and attacked. They traded four strikes, five, and then Silth's blade looped around Ni-Ko's and tore the Shield of Light out of her hand. It turned end over end through the air and buried its point in the ground in front of Dais.

Silth's blade opened two lines across Ni-Ko's face, then one across her chest.

Dais knew the law of the Shield as well as he knew his own name. Only the clan's leader could hold it. Anyone else who tried might not survive.

He grabbed the handle.

Pain went through him like wildfire, every nerve lit at once. The air left his lungs. His knees buckled, and the edges of the world went white. He didn't let go. He tore the sword out of the ground, screamed his sister's name, and threw it.

Silth was already lunging for Ni-Ko's heart. Ni-Ko caught the sword and turned the thrust, but not far enough. Silth's blade went into her stomach and through her, until its point touched the earth behind her.

Ni-Ko tried to raise her sword arm. Silth caught her wrist and held it, gently. She looked into Ni-Ko's eyes and hushed her, softly, the way a mother hushes a child to sleep. Ni-Ko's body went slack and slid down the blade to the ground.

Silth looked at Dais. She raised her bow and sent a single arrow past his face, so close it opened his right cheek. Then she and her sword broke into motes of light that streamed past him and gathered on Aleric's shoulder, where she sat again, small now, like a doll.

"I suppose I'll be seeing you around, young swordsman," Aleric said.

Lines of light began to draw themselves at his feet, a five-pointed star inside a circle. When the pentacle closed, a pillar of blue fire leaped from the ground to the sky, and Aleric and Silth faded into it.

Dais had no idea where it went. He knew only that he was going in after them. He picked up one of Jacob's swords and walked to the edge of the light.

"Wait."

It was barely a whisper. Ni-Ko was on her knees, one arm stretched toward him, the Shield of Light lying across her palm.

"You can't kill him with that," she said, "and you know it." She pushed the sword at him. "I name you, Dais, High Priest of the Clerics. You're the last of us now. The Key is your duty. Yours alone."

He took the sword from her hand, and she fell.

This time the handle didn't burn. Its power ran into him like a current and settled in his bones as if it had always belonged there. Dais turned, gripped the Shield of Light, and dove headfirst into the light.

He never looked back.

Chapter One: The Black Rain

Earth, 2316

Sightings

The sky over the Great Wall of China changed in the space of a breath. Orange went to magenta. Magenta went to red. The red deepened until, for one long moment, the whole sky was the color of dried blood.

Then it tore.

It began as a hairline, a thin dark seam drawn across the heavens, and it spread into a wide black smile that swallowed the clouds on either side. Over Moscow it was happening too. Over New York it was happening too.

Something pushed out of the dark.

At first it looked like the prow of an old sailing ship nosing out of a bank of smoke. As more of it came, it looked less like a ship and more like the head of an impossible lizard: a bony chin, nostrils like tunnel mouths, eyes closed as if it were asleep. Its neck went on and on before it reached a chest plated in scales the size of manhole covers. It had no forelegs. In their place were wings that spanned twice the length of a football field, and behind it trailed a tail as thick as a bus at the root and a hundred feet long.

It didn't beat its wings. It glided, as calm as a blimp. A second one came out behind it, and around them, in looping flocks like starlings, came dozens of smaller ones. Each of the small ones had a rider.

The Black Rain

Above New York

The Black Rain. That was what they would call it, for generations.

The smaller dragons drew patterns in the sky, and black smoke poured from their mouths, and for a while the people of New York only stood in the streets and stared. They held up their phones. Kids sat on their parents' shoulders. It was the most beautiful thing any of them had ever seen.

Then came the voice. It hummed through the air like distant thunder, a chant with no source, coming from nowhere and everywhere at once, as if God himself were whispering violently into every ear in the city.

The chant quickened. The black clouds rumbled. And then it rained.

The silence broke into screaming, because the rain wasn't rain. It was blades: thin, pencil-sized daggers with razor points, falling out of the clouds. A sprinkle at first. Then a downpour. People ran for doorways and subway stairs as the blades came down in sheets, slicing through anyone in the open, starring windshields, punching through the canopies of hover cars and the windows of the transit lanes above the street, burying themselves in the asphalt up to the hilt.

Then the boulders began to fall.

They tumbled from the backs of the two great dragons, gray masses the size of delivery trucks. They smashed cars flat and caved in rooftops and sank into the concrete like baseballs into mud, and the blade-rain kept falling on and around them.

Then the boulders moved.

They unrolled themselves like pill bugs, except that there was no soft underside. They were stone all the way through. When they stood, they were giants, fifteen and twenty and twenty-five feet tall, in the smoking ruins of Manhattan. Some carried clubs of rock as big as tree trunks. Others had only their hands: mammoth hands with four thick fingers.

For a moment they stood perfectly still while the razor rain pattered off their shoulders like hail off the hood of a car. Their faces had no mouths. Their eyes were empty caves under a heavy shelf of brow.

Then, all at once, red sparks woke in the dark of those eyes, and the golems began to walk.

They went through buildings as if the walls were curtains. They stepped on hover cars. They swatted police cruisers out of the air. The police opened up with their light-phasers, and the beams splashed off the golems like the blades in the rain. It was a massacre.

The invasion had begun.

The governments of the world answered faster than anyone had a right to expect. Ordinary police couldn't hold the golems or survive the rain, but the Riot Control Units could put up more of a fight. An RCU was a handful of robotic A.R.M.S., the armored robotic mobile suits that had become the backbone of every army on the planet, fitted for crowd work with plasma throwers, sonic grenades, small impact missiles and heavy phase machine guns. Behind them came armored foot soldiers carrying everything from sidearms to shoulder cannons.

Every one of them in riot transport DB1249 was thinking the same thing. We are about to fight rocks that shouldn't exist, and we don't know if they can die.

The transport's doors dropped open like a landing craft's at Normandy. The first thing the team saw, and for most of them the last, was an ogre of solid stone coming at them at a dead run with a club the size of a tree. It hit the open doorway like a runaway train.

Transport DB1249 exploded.

They were the lucky ones. They would not see the years of bloodshed to come.

Might of the Dragons

"We have to... have to take out those damn clouds!" The transmission crackled and clicked. "We're dead down here if that rain keeps up!"

It cut off, and a new voice filled the channel, clear and loud.

"You heard the man, Rangers! Let's get up there and show these things, whatever the hell they are, how we say welcome to Earth."

That was Air Captain John Morris, commander of the Bravo Rangers aerial strike force, and his voice came over the cockpit speakers like a lion's roar.

The Rangers flew Wings: non-humanoid A.R.M.S. built like huge metal horseshoe crabs, silver and white, with laser cannons slung under each wing and pulse guns in the nose.

"Whatever, Cap." The next voice was a woman's, low and rough, talking around a cigar. "Let's just haul ass before the Wraiths show up and steal all our glory. You know what a show-off that Roy Hunter is."

Meg Jordan. Call sign Smoker. Plenty of people swore she was the best pilot alive. She would have had the title, too, if not for the man she'd just named: Captain Roy F. Hunter of the Wraith Squadron.

The chatter died as the Rangers reached the edge of the storm.

They flew into it.

From a distance the smaller dragons had looked like insects carrying toy soldiers. Up close, nothing was small. The smallest dragons were fifteen to twenty feet from nose to tail, with wingspans twice that. The toy soldiers were huge, barbarian-looking men and women with spears and longbows. Only the size of the two great dragons, still drifting forward like flying fortresses, made anything else seem small. Their shadows over the city were the darkest any of the pilots had ever seen.

As the Rangers bore down on the lead giant, a dozen of the smaller dragons broke off from their smoke patterns and came to meet them in tight formation. One rider drew a spear from the quiver on his saddle and held the tip near his dragon's jaw. The dragon coughed a small ball of flame, and the spearhead caught. Down the line, the other riders did the same with arrows.

"Brace for incoming!" Morris called.

A shower of burning spears and arrows came up at them, and the Rangers broke formation to scatter.

Someone laughed on the open channel. "C'mon, Cap. It's sticks. What are they gonna do?"

The answer came a second later. An arrow clipped one of the Wings as it rolled away from another, and the arrow and the aircraft went up together in a single fireball.

The Bravo Rangers opened fire.

It was a brutal fight, and many pilots died in it, but they broke the smoke dragons long enough to stop the rain. For a few minutes the sky over Manhattan was only sky, and the ground forces pulled out everyone they could reach.

Chapter Two: The Black Dragon Rider

Fort Red Rock

While the great dragons and the golems took the cities, smaller rifts were opening all over the world, in places nobody would have thought worth attacking.

"All right, rodents!"

The voice was easy but it carried. It held a hangar full of very tired soldiers, a few hours off a twelve-week tour on the line. Some of them leaned against their A.R.M.S. units, eight-ton walking machines in desperate need of service. And cleaning. Mostly cleaning.

That was exactly what Lieutenant Chris Steadfield was doing when the captain started in: working a grease rag over his Series 12 Aerial, one of the unit's three. The Twelves were built for the sky. Where the others could only hop and hover, a Twelve could fold itself from walker to flyer in a few seconds and stay in a dogfight longer than it could stay in a ground fight. They gave the walkers cover from above. The wings of the Mongoose, the captain called them. Steadfield's call sign was Gunner.

"As of today," said Captain Terrie Smiles, "we are released from active duty. Twelve weeks on the line, and today we go home."

"For all of two weeks!" someone called out.

David Wrangler. Call sign Speed. The new kid. He'd been sealed in his cockpit too long, and he'd gone a little stir-crazy the way rookies did. Terrie let it go. Mostly.

"Two weeks!" Terrie said, walking the line. "You'd better be grateful for two weeks. That's how you tell a real war from this bullshit."

He let that sit. Terrie knew a real war. He'd been sixteen when Angus Rose put him on a fast boat in the Pacific and pointed it at the last GIA Protocol, the last of the thinking machines from the Endless War. He remembered the old man's voice on the comm, All right, worms, listen up, and he remembered the mines, and the boat that had been beside theirs and then wasn't. Eighteen years later he still called his own people rodents, and not one of them knew where he'd gotten it.

"Border spats," he said. "Corporate land grabs. They send us out to fight, then they give us a vacation, then we fight again. A war with vacations." He shook his head. "Sometimes I wonder, guys." A long pause. "But ours is not to question why..."

The whole hangar answered him like a choir. "Ours is but to do or die!"

The laughter that followed was loud and real.

Terrie smiled. He knew what they were thinking, and he'd always found the best way to handle it was to say it out loud first. His officers called him Captain Smiles, and he was good-natured and patient and fair. Nobody who'd served under him mistook that for softness. The Grey Mongoose had the highest success rate in the West Federated Armies, and not because Terrie Smiles was good at negotiating.

"Captain Smiles! Captain Smiles!"

A communications officer came across the hangar floor at a full sprint, as if he'd run there from the other side of the planet.

"Whoa, whoa. Breathe, son." Gunner stepped into his path two steps before he would have flattened the captain, still holding the grease rag.

"What's the problem?" Terrie asked, calmly. But the fear in the boy's eyes was the kind that spread, and Terrie felt some of it settle in his own stomach. The officer handed him a printout.

"I don't know if it's real or someone's idea of a joke, sir. But I keep getting these reports. Dragons. Flying lizards. And they're coming in from everywhere."

Terrie read it twice. Some part of him wanted to laugh. A deeper part told him to move. Before he could decide which one to listen to, the base siren went off.

A crack ran across the pale sky above Fort Red Rock like lightning that stayed. Out of it came dragons, swarms of them, and they were carrying rocks. Some boulders were so big it took two or three dragons together to lift them. There was no smoke this time and no rain of blades. Only stone and fire, pouring out of the sky as though the world had ended and the heavens were finally coming down.

Terrie didn't think. Over the siren they could all hear it now: the heavy leather slap of enormous wings, and the roar of fire.

"A.R.M.S. UP!" There was nothing easy in his voice now. "Brace for incoming!"

Every suit in the unit was manned and powered before the first stone landed. It hit the bunker next door, and the building folded in on itself. Terrie heard screaming and secondary explosions, and knew that if they were going to make a fight of it they had to get outside, now.

He dropped his canopy and called Gunner. "We're going out shooting. Wait for my signal, then get those wings in the air. You hear me?"

"Loud and clear." Gunner said it like he wasn't afraid, like his heart wasn't going like a war drum. He relayed the order to his two wingmen and folded his Twelve into flight mode. Hovering a foot off the hangar floor, he waited.

The Mongoose blew the hangar doors off their tracks and charged out into the fire.

Fort Red Rock was rubble and flame. It looked like a painting, one of the old ones of London burning, the night full of dancing embers. But there were more than embers in that sky. Wave after wave of dragons were pouring fire down on the base.

Terrie was good, and his people were good, and they came out swinging. Their suits had just come off three months of combat and should have been powered down for service that afternoon. Terrie had never let a suit go cold in his life, not even on leave. Every one of them was locked, loaded and ready.

The Grey Mongoose ran twelve A.R.M.S.:

  • Three Series 12 Aerials, the flyers.

  • Eight Series 10 Advanced Ground Units, the A.G.U.s.

  • One K.O.T.R. Unit 15, an officer's suit, piloted by Terrie Smiles.

The golems were still falling out of the sky as the dragons burned everything around them. Four of the A.G.U.s went straight at the golems already on the ground. The rest put their guns on the air, on the dragons and on the golems that hadn't landed yet. Terrie dropped two more of the stone things and yelled for Gunner, and the three Twelves were already climbing before he finished the word.

Help had come from a neighboring outpost, and the sky was crowded with fighters. The dragons were tearing the slower ones apart. Others hung on stubbornly. Gunner and his wingmen stayed low and close, covering the Mongoose as it tried to shove the giants back to whatever hell they'd come from.

On the ground, it was working. Grenade rounds cracked golems open like eggs, and the heat-seekers found dragons overhead. But the enemy had numbers, and the numbers were getting worse. For every golem they dropped, five marines died. The base was beginning to look like a lost cause. That didn't stop them from holding it.

Two hundred marines had been stationed at Fort Red Rock. In less than an hour there were seventy. Terrie watched a boulder land on an anti-aircraft emplacement, crush both gunners, then uncurl and charge one of his A.G.U.s.

He put two grenades into the golems coming up behind him without looking and went. The grenades went off at his back. Full thrust, gun leveled, he let the targeting reticle crawl across the moving rock until it locked, and fired. A small impact missile leaped from the barrel and turned the entire left side of the golem to dust. The marine it had been charging turned around just in time to be showered with pebbles.

The Swordsman Appears

The sky emptied out until Gunner and his two wingmen were the only things up there that weren't reptiles. With the fighters gone, the dragons turned their fire on the ground.

Gunner wasn't having it. He went into the thick of them and painted the clouds red with dragon blood. Somewhere in that fight, both of his wingmen went down. Now he was alone, thirty to one. Better than the two hundred to one we started with, he thought.

"Coming in hot with five on my tail!" he called.

"Bring them to me," Terrie said. "I'll handle the rest."

Gunner flew like the devil was on his ass. Fireballs flashed past him, in front of him, behind him, trailing streams of flame. One grazed his left wing, and it started to smoke.

Terrie saw him coming and set his feet. Seven dragons were on Gunner now. "I've got you, G-man. When you pass me, dip low. Got it?"

A proximity alarm screamed in his cockpit.

Terrie twisted just in time to see the golem before it hit him and slammed his suit to the ground. His shot went into the dirt. Lying on his back, he watched Gunner scream past overhead, left wing trailing smoke, and knew that the miss had just killed him.

Gunner dipped, as ordered. No shot came. He hauled the stick back and climbed, and a fireball caught the left wing again, and the climb turned into a spin. Seven dragons beat their great wings and rose after him.

Terrie destroyed the golem on top of him and rolled to his feet, firing. He caught the last of the seven. The rest climbed out of his range. They were closing on Gunner's spinning ship when a jet of blue fire crossed the sky and burned the two leaders to cinders. They fell like meteors.

Terrie traced the blue fire back to its source: a black dragon, bigger than any of the others, with someone standing on its back. It came on with lightning speed. Three of the four remaining dragons broke off to meet it, and three fireballs met them and swallowed them, and they fell too.

Gunner's ship had climbed through the clouds. First a buzzer, then an alarm, and then the whole cockpit was howling. He tried everything he knew. Nothing answered.

There was a moment of perfect calm in the middle of it. Above the clouds, the sky was the clearest he had ever seen. The stars were diamond dust thrown across black silk.

Then the ship rolled over, and he was falling, straight toward the last of the dragons. It flared its wings, arched its back, and drew a long breath.

The black dragon's jaws closed on its neck.

It shook the limp body like a cat with a snake and flung it toward the ground. Then it dove after Gunner, caught his ship in its hind claws a few hundred feet above the dirt, and set it down beside Terrie's suit as gently as a crate of eggs. Terrie hadn't moved the whole time. His guns were trained on the black dragon.

They looked at each other across the scorched field, the man in the machine and the man on the dragon.

The rider stood perfectly still from the waist up. Only his knees moved, riding the beat of the dragon's wings. A belt crossed his hips with a large red crystal at its center, and two swords hung from it, clearly not made as a pair. One was long and slender, with a small, plain guard like a samurai's and a handle the color of the smoke drifting across the field. "It's been hard on everybody," Gunner said. "I lost two of my best friends yesterday. But I can only imagine what you're carrying. My captain, Terrie, he and I are close." He looked off, lost in it, and gave a small laugh. "He's kind of my hero, you know? I respect him that much."

She knew exactly what he meant. Morris had been hers. She'd outscored him on nearly every course they'd ever flown, but he'd been a born leader, and a good teacher. He'd taught her the rules of staying alive.

"There's something Terrie told me a long time ago," Gunner said. "He told me to remember it if he ever got killed."

"What?"

"That heroes are meant to die." He said it plainly. "It's a hero's last duty to give birth to new heroes. When a hero dies, they leave behind hope, and the will to make sure it wasn't for nothing." He looked at her. "That's the most heroic thing there is."

Meg smiled at him, at Chris, at Gunner, and was proud to know him.

He turned and started back to his ship.

"Hey, Gunner!"

He stopped.

"Thanks."

He waved without turning around. "Don't mention it. You can thank me in the sky tonight."

They would leave for Florida an hour before sunset.

Chapter Six: The Dome

The Briefing

Half an hour before launch, the whole strike force crowded into the briefing hall, and the man at the podium was Dr. Mason Grant, the most celebrated scientist and inventor in the Federated States.

"A few hours ago, we tested a theory of mine," he said. "The results were mixed." He cleared his throat and leaned on the podium. "The dome protecting what we're calling the Arc is an extremely powerful magnetic field. But I believe it has a limit.

"When we first found it, we had a satellite put a concentrated laser on it. At first, nothing. We kept the beam on. After a few minutes, the dome began to change color. Its glow faded. It weakened, enough for us to get a few blurry pictures of what's underneath. But before it broke, the satellite's laser overheated, and we had to shut it down or lose the satellite."

He stepped away from the podium to a wall of display panels, and the lights went down. One panel lit with a map of Florida, a bright dot pulsing at its southern tip. The other showed five grainy photographs.

"These were taken while the shield was at its weakest. Less than five minutes later it was back at full strength. But we did weaken it. In three and a half hours, the satellite will be ready to fire again. If, at that exact moment, at least five heavy tanks with anti-magnetic beam cannons put their fire on the dome together with the satellite, it should break. That will give the air and ground units about five minutes to get inside before it comes back up."

He found Terrie in the crowd. "Captain Smiles, you'll be responsible for protecting the tanks and getting them there."

Before he let them go, Grant walked them through the new modifications to the Mongoose's suits. Gunner's in particular. Gunner had been given the newest and most dangerous Aerial unit in existence: fusion beams, lasers, plasma guns, four kinds of missiles, and an A.I. system with an anti-impact guard that would steer the ship clear of collisions on its own. It also came with two remote A.I. gunships of its own, Wing-Guns, to fly in place of the wingmen he'd lost.

Gunner looked at the A.I. specs for a long moment. Everyone in the room was old enough to remember what the history books said about thinking machines. Then he signed for it.

The Drop

From a distance, the dome looked like a glowing blister on the Florida coast. It was green, and it was as big as a football stadium, and it sat on the beach inside a ring of creatures so thick they looked like a second shoreline. The sky above it was dark with what looked like a storm cloud, until you saw it shift and turn like a flock of birds. It was dragons. Hundreds of them, with riders. And something else among them, smaller, but by no means small.

The convoy filled the sky behind them, two hundred ships at least. Most were unmanned drones. Forty-five or so had people in them: fighters, bombers, drop ships. One of the drop ships carried the Grey Mongoose.

Before they reached the drop zone, the ground erupted with arrows and fireballs. Ships began to burst all around them, and the drop ship carrying the Mongoose took three hits and began to spin.

Terrie and his people blew their way out through the side of it. They burst out of the falling hull in a cloud of smoke and debris, caught themselves on their boosters, and dove for the beach, firing at the golems and dragons below. They came down like burning boulders until the last instant, when the retro-thrusters kicked in and set them on the sand. Nobody was hurt.

They hit the ground running and gunning. They were surrounded, and they had to clear a landing zone for the tanks: ten armored robotic vehicles carrying the anti-magnetic cannons. Terrie needed at least five of them to reach the dome.

He called the rest of the drop fleet down. The Mongoose and the other ground units dug in and held a perimeter while the drop ships fought their way in. He got Gunner on the comm.

"Gunner, I need you and your wings on the drop ships carrying the tanks. We cannot lose those tanks!"

He hadn't realized he was shouting. Gunner was already gone.

His onboard computer flagged the two drop ships carrying the tanks, and he sent his Wing-Guns to guard them. The two little gunships flew off on their own and attacked anything that went near either ship. Gunner took the rest of the sky.

It was fast and savage. On the ground, the marines fought foot soldiers, golems, riders on wingless dragons, and then dragons that were nothing but teeth. The wingless ones were the worst. Their white-hot fangs went through A.R.M.S. armor like a hot knife through butter. In the air, the fighters tangled with the dragon riders and with the insect-like dragon-men called dragonets.

Inch by inch, Terrie's ground forces pushed the tanks up the beach to the base of the dome. Once the tanks were in place, Terrie would give the word, Dr. Grant would fire the satellite, and with luck, the dome would come down.

The base of the dome was guarded by golems and small dragons and a few dozen foot soldiers in armor that made them look twice their size. They lost four tanks on the way in. By the time Terrie had the rest in position, monsters had them surrounded on every side, and the marines fought like animals to keep them off the tanks.

Terrie called Dr. Grant. Grant had bad news. The satellite was taking longer than expected to recharge. They would have to hold.

It felt like forever. They lost two more tanks. When Grant finally gave the order to fire, four were left out of ten. One fewer than he had said they'd need.

A light came down from heaven like a beam of yellow sunlight and lit up the flocks of dragons circling the dome. It widened, and then narrowed, burning the dragons in its path to cinders, and drove down onto the dome with titanic force. Terrie gave the order, and four beams leaped from the tanks and struck the dome at once.

At first nothing happened. Then the green glow began to turn red. The dome swelled like a balloon about to burst.

It went with a force that threw every soldier near it, Kabrean and human alike, thirty or forty feet back across the sand.

The marines moved in. The air support came in behind them.

The Heavens Were Riddled with Death

All the while the ground forces had been fighting their way to the dome, the air units had been in a war of their own. The dragonets didn't look dangerous at a distance. Up close, their numbers could swamp a ship. Once they got their claws in, they tore the hull apart looking for whoever was inside, and when they found someone, they ate them, like sharks in a frenzy. Around them flew the dragon riders, and dragons the size of bombers. Everything in that sky was fighting to stay alive.

Neither side had the edge. The Kabreans had numbers. The humans had firepower.

Roy and Smoker were flying side by side for the first time, and they were something to see. That's not to take anything from the Wraiths, who lived up to their name with tight formations and perfectly timed strikes. But Roy and Smoker were a team in a different way. They were competing with each other, a strange and deadly game, and between them they danced a ballet of guns and fire across the clouds.

Smoker saw the dome go down first. She called it to Roy and the rest of the fighters. The door was open. Time to go in for the kill.

Stepping into Hell

Terrie's people pushed past the dazed front guards and into the dome, and found a new problem waiting for them: a gray dragon fifty or sixty feet tall.

Beyond it, maybe a hundred and fifty yards off, stood a platform ringed by enormous stone gargoyles, all of them kneeling. In the middle of the platform rose an arched ivory tower crowned with a huge red crystal.

At first the gray dragon didn't look alive. It looked like a statue, or a building with smoke drifting from it. Now it spread its wings and arched its long neck like a cobra about to strike, and its great eyes moved over the marines below. Smoke rolled out of its nostrils as though a furnace burned somewhere deep inside it.

It drew its head back and breathed in. Then its head shot forward, and fire poured from its jaws at terrible speed. The fire swallowed one of the heavy tanks and every A.R.M.S. unit around it. A moment later, the tank exploded.

The dragon stepped back and drew another long breath. Terrie watched its chest swell and start to glow yellow, then brighter. He understood. Was it too late?

He didn't bother giving the order. If their training held, his people would follow their captain's lead, and they did. Everyone left poured fire into the glowing chest, trying to chip through the scales. It was working, but not fast enough. The great head began to swing forward again, blue flame leaking between its teeth. They were finished.

Terrie and a few others fired missiles. The dragon clapped its wings together and blew them off course with one gust, so hard it spun some suits around and set them firing into their own lines.

As the wings swept back and bared the dragon's chest again, a flash of light crossed it. It was a sword, burying itself to the hilt. Terrie looked up and saw Dais standing on the back of his black dragon, arms flung wide to either side, chanting.

The gray dragon reeled and swung its head to find him. The sword in its chest began to glow, and then it exploded, and the dragon exploded with it. When the smoke cleared, Terrie looked up and found Dais looking back down at him.

Past where the gray dragon had stood rose the tower, twenty stories at least. When Terrie zoomed in, he could see a man at its base in a blood-red robe, his arms raised over his head.

"That's got to be him, sir," Gunner said over the comm.

Dais answered on the comm Terrie had slipped him in the cell. "That's him. And I'll tell you now, none of you can beat him. Leave Aleric to me."

Terrie didn't like it, and Dais could tell. "Listen to me. Aleric is one of the most powerful wizards in all of Kabrick. He's worth more than any army. I don't know if I can take him with my sword, but I know he'll crush you. Stay back and cover me. Please."

Terrie looked down and decided it was a good plan, because his hands were about to be full anyway. An army of golems and huge dragon-men was coming straight at his unit.

Gunner took his new ship up, and Dais took his dragon up beside him. The moment they did, most of the dragon-men took to the air to meet them. Seconds later the air support arrived, with Roy and Smoker leading it, and they carpet-bombed the ground forces and cleared a road to the wizard.

Terrie and his marines charged.

Chapter Seven: The King of Stone

The Gargoyles

Aleric stood among his kneeling gargoyles and waited for his guests.

A few moments before the marines arrived, he lifted his cane. The stone serpent wound down its length, and its jaws were closed around a glowing red crystal. He raised it over his head and murmured something, and a bolt of light leaped from the crystal on the cane to the great crystal on top of the tower. That one began to glow as well, faintly, and then brighter, and brighter still.

Terrie saw it and knew they had to bring that tower down, fast. Between him and it stood the gargoyles, twenty to thirty feet tall with long claws and folded stone wings. Terrie wanted them destroyed before they could become anything else.

"Take out the statues as you go!"

He put two rockets into the nearest one. They burst against its flank and didn't leave a mark. As if the shots had woken them, the gargoyles' eyes began to light up one after another, like a city coming back on after a blackout, until a sea of red eyes looked out at the marines in the dark.

The one he'd hit was very much alive now, and coming for him, wings spread. Terrie fired two impact grenades into the wings, and they shattered. The gargoyle stumbled back and looked at the stumps, as if it didn't understand. Then its face filled with rage.

It lifted its arms and roared, so loud the ground shook, and every shard of its broken wings rose into the air around it. It looked at Terrie, and the shards came at him like bullets. He boosted hard left and shot down the pieces he couldn't dodge.

When the air was clear, he went for the head, hoping it was a weak spot. He fired four heavy concussion grenades. The first three blew apart some of the chunks still orbiting the gargoyle, and they fell to the sand as dust. The fourth hit its forearm, which burst, and then drew itself back together out of the floating stone.

Terrie and his marines kept fighting.

Dais and Gunner fought their way to the edge of the platform. Dais jumped from his dragon's back, shouted something in a language none of them knew, and landed with a thud that shook the ground and threw up a cloud of dust. When it cleared, he was down on one knee, and the long sword in his hand was blazing blue. The short red one was gone, spent in the gray dragon's heart. Glowing symbols had appeared all along the blade.

Terrie had never seen him look so focused. His eyes were fixed on Aleric, and the battle around him seemed to fall away. He charged in a straight line, cutting as he went. Every gargoyle his sword touched simply crumbled to dust. He was a one-man demolition crew, carving through stone and anything else in his way, closer and closer to the man in red.

With enough work, the gargoyles could be beaten. Terrie, Gunner and the rest pushed forward to back Dais up.

Aleric

Dais stopped twenty feet from the hooded man, who was hunched over his cane like an old man.

"Cut the shit, Aleric. I know age means nothing to something like you. You can drop the feeble old man act." He walked forward. "I've waited a long time for this, old man. Don't disappoint me."

Aleric straightened, and kept straightening, until he stood almost twice as tall as he'd seemed. He pushed the hood back from a bald head. A strange metal cap covered the crown of it and came to a point between his eyes. He was smiling, the kind of smile a loan shark smiles when he finally gets his hands on someone who owes him: greedy and hungry, creasing a face that looked no older than middle age.

His voice was deep, almost bottomless. "I've been waiting for you, my young prodigy. I was beginning to wonder what was keeping you." He took a step. "How you've grown. And you've mastered the sword. You've even surpassed Ni-Ko. Very good. Very, very good indeed."

"Enough talk! You killed my family, the only thing that ever meant anything to me. And then you took her from me. You killed her!"

"Hmm. Hmm, ha. Ha, ha, ha, HA!" Aleric laughed from the belly. "You think I killed her? That's grand. My boy, you are very confused." The laughter stopped. "But none of that matters now. What matters is that you've set your heart on taking my life. Unfortunately, this is not the time, and this is not the place, and you are not the man."

He turned half away. "I had hoped you would come to me wiser. But you're only human, after all. We'll speak again soon, Dais. For now, show me what you've learned with that blade."

He set his staff, and beckoned.

Dais charged, wrapped in the glow of his sword. When the staff met the blade, the shock rolled out across the beach, and Terrie felt it through his suit fifty yards away. Dais gripped the hilt with both hands and leaned in, trying to throw the wizard off balance. He couldn't. Aleric was too fast. In seconds they were locked together, trading blows at close quarters.

Most people believe wizards aren't fighters, that those who know so much of the mind know nothing of the body. It isn't true. Body and mind are one, and to master one you must master both. Aleric had. He fought with his body and his staff and with the earth itself. The pale stones scattered across the beach glowed faintly, and at his will they rose to fight beside him.

Dais fought like a man possessed. His sword cut through the pink light of the tower, which hung over them like a dim lighthouse on the shore.

Then Aleric turned a cut, swept his leg, and put him on the ground. As Dais struggled up, he saw the crystal on the staff burn red, and a pink beam leaped from it and swallowed him, sword and all.

His skin began to burn, and the burning raced through every cell of his body. He went down on his knees, still gripping the sword, fighting it. Aleric took the staff in both hands and leaned into it, forcing more and more power into him. Dais weakened. It looked like he would break.

Then his sword began to glow blue. Faintly at first, and then violently, until it formed a bubble of blue light inside the red one. Aleric strained to hold it. Straining with everything he had, Dais began to stand, the blue pushing back the red. He made it to his feet, and then he began to weaken again.

He brought the sword up in front of him, pointed at Aleric, in a stance somewhere between a batter waiting on a pitch and a samurai ready to strike. Before he could swing, Aleric's eyes blazed, and the beam flared into a blinding red flash. It rolled over Dais like a tidal wave, and when it washed away, there was only a statue.

It was Dais in perfect likeness, his hair and clothes caught mid-motion, frozen in his warrior's stance, ready to strike.

Aleric began to rise into the air, floating up toward the crystal on top of the Arc.

Gunner saw all of it from his cockpit, and watched in horror as Dais turned to stone. He broke out of the air fight and dove to help, flying like a bat out of hell, shooting as he went and leaving laser scars where dragons and golems had been. Terrie yelled at him over the comm. Gunner didn't answer. He kept flying. Behind him, Terrie's marines had fought their way through the gargoyles and destroyed most of them.

The statue of Dais stood lifeless in its fierce stance. Then the sword began to glow. The symbols etched into the blade lit up bright blue, and the light spread until it covered the statue, and then the statue burst. Stone fragments flew in every direction.

When the smoke cleared, Dais was standing there in the flesh. His eyes went to the top of the tower, where Aleric sat on the great crystal, smiling down at him. Dais leaped into the air after him.

Silth

Out of the corner of his eye, Dais saw a spark of light streaking at him with hellish speed. He tried to twist away. Too late. There was a blinding flash, and he shut his eyes against it, and something hit him and pushed him aside. He fell and rolled and came up on his feet, and saw what had hit him.

His black dragon lay on her side with a steaming arrow in her chest.

He started toward her and two more arrows drove him back. He rolled clear and looked up, and saw who it was. He should have known.

"Silth," Dais said, with pure rage. "Aleric's little fairy whore."

She hung in the air like a hummingbird, in skintight black leather, her ears long and fine, her skin the color of pure silk.

"I should have known you'd be here," he said. He touched the scar on his cheek and remembered the sting of her arrow.

She smiled. "It's good to see you, Dais, sweetie. I hope you're not too mad at me for putting your little dragon down. I think she was getting old anyway." She laughed.

Dais looked up. Aleric was gone. But Dais knew where he was going, and he would deal with him when the time came. Right now there was a debt to settle with this little fairy bitch. He could see it as plain as day: Silth's sword buried in his sister, Ni-Ko's body sliding down the blade to the ground. For a moment he forgot Aleric completely. Silth needed to be handled.

Her translucent wings blurred behind her as she loosed three arrows at once. Dais knocked them aside without effort. He was moving fast, and before she knew it they were blade to blade. Her sword was long and slim like a fencer's and glowed a pale white. Metal and magic clashed, evenly matched, and every strike threw sparks into the dark like fireworks. But Dais had the edge, because he had his rage.

Terrie's voice came over the comm as the marines arrived. "Stand back. We'll cover you."

Before Dais could tell them to keep away, Silth fired an arrow into the sand in front of one of the Mongoose A.G.U.s. Thorned vines exploded out of the ground where it struck, wrapped the suit, and crushed it with the soldier still inside.

Another arrow was already flying straight at Terrie. Dais leaped and threw a dart, and it burst against the arrow in midair. When he turned back to Silth, there was only a blinding light, and then something like a tiny shooting star, streaking away in the direction the crystal faced.

Then came the wave.

The crystal atop the Arc let loose a massive beam that raced across the dark ocean toward the Bermuda Triangle. When the beam faded, the crystal crumbled to dust, and the tower came down after it. Every stone soldier on the beach crumbled where it stood, and the dragoons lifted off and flew away after the beam.

Terrie, Dais and Gunner stood together on the beach in silence, looking out over the dark ocean.
